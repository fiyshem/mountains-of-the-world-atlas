The Caucasus Mountains lie between the Black Sea and Caspian Sea, spanning parts of Russia, Georgia, Azerbaijan, and nearby regions. The Greater Caucasus contains high peaks, glaciers, deep valleys, and isolated mountain habitats.

The region has a distinctive mixture of European and Asian biogeographic influences. Elevation, rainfall, and the contrast between northern and southern slopes produce diverse forest and alpine communities.

## Mammals

- East Caucasian tur (Capra cylindricornis): a wild mountain goat native to the eastern Greater Caucasus.
- West Caucasian tur (Capra caucasica): a mountain ungulate associated with the western Greater Caucasus.
- Caucasian red deer (Cervus elaphus populations): deer living in suitable forest and mountain landscapes.
- Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx): a predator of forested and mountainous habitats.
- Brown bear (Ursus arctos): an omnivore that uses varied habitats according to food availability.
- Chamois (Rupicapra rupicapra): found in suitable rocky and alpine habitats.

## Birds

- Caucasian black grouse (Lyrurus mlokosiewiczi): a specialised mountain grouse associated with subalpine and alpine vegetation.
- Caucasian snowcock (Tetraogallus caucasicus): a high-altitude bird of open, rocky slopes.
- Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos): a raptor that uses open mountain terrain for hunting.
- Bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus): a scavenger associated with mountainous regions.
- Caucasian chiffchaff (Phylloscopus lorenzii): a small songbird associated with wooded mountain habitats.

## Plants

- Oriental beech (Fagus orientalis).
- Caucasian fir (Abies nordmanniana).
- Caucasian spruce (Picea orientalis).
- Rhododendrons and other mountain shrubs.
- Alpine grasses, herbs, and flowering plants.

The Caucasus is an important region for studying endemism, refugia, and the role of mountain barriers in evolution. Fragmented habitat, illegal hunting, infrastructure, and changing climate conditions create conservation challenges.

## A crossroads of habitats

The Caucasus is a useful study area for the role of mountain barriers in biodiversity. A high ridge can redirect weather, isolate populations and create different habitats on opposite slopes. Lower forests, subalpine scrub, open alpine grassland and glaciated peaks may occur close together, but their distributions depend on exposure, precipitation and local geology. A species associated with one section of the range should not automatically be assumed to occur in every Caucasus valley.

The region’s combination of European and Asian biogeographic influences makes it especially interesting for comparative ecology. Localised species and distinctive populations can persist where suitable habitat remains connected and disturbance is limited. At the same time, steep terrain can make populations naturally fragmented, so the scale and location of conservation work matter.

## Questions for a regional study

Begin with the ridge line and ask where water is carried by prevailing winds. Compare the vegetation of opposing slopes, then look for the transition from forest to open high ground. Investigate how roads, settlements, pasture, hunting pressure and protected-area boundaries may affect movement by large mammals and mountain birds. A robust assessment combines field observations with regional research and reliable species records rather than treating the mountain system as ecologically uniform.
