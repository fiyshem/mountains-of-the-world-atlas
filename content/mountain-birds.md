Mountain birds are useful for studying flight, migration, habitat specialisation, and the effects of environmental change.

## 6.1 Birds of alpine cliffs

Species such as alpine choughs, snowcocks, wallcreepers, and some raptors use cliffs and rocky slopes for nesting, roosting, or foraging.

Cliffs provide ledges and cavities that may be inaccessible to many ground predators. However, disturbance from climbing, infrastructure, and repeated human activity can affect some breeding sites.

## 6.2 Forest birds

Mountain forests support woodpeckers, owls, thrushes, tits, flycatchers, and many other groups.

Woodpeckers can create cavities that are later used by other species. Insect-eating birds help transfer energy from invertebrates to higher trophic levels, while fruit-eating birds can disperse seeds.

Forest structure matters: old trees, dead wood, understory vegetation, and connected canopy can all affect which species occupy a site.

## 6.3 Migratory mountain birds

Some birds breed at high elevations during warmer months and move to lower elevations or different geographic regions during winter.

Migration allows them to exploit seasonal food availability, but routes can be affected by weather, habitat loss, and changing climate. Not all mountain birds migrate long distances; some remain in the same region throughout the year.

## 6.4 Mountain wetlands and river birds

High-altitude streams, lakes, and marshes support specialised birds such as dippers, torrent ducks, waterfowl, and shorebirds, depending on the region.

Cold, oxygen-rich streams can support distinctive aquatic invertebrates and fish communities. Pollution, altered streamflow, dams, and habitat disturbance can affect the birds that depend on them.

## 6.5 How to build a mountain bird directory

For each species, include:

- Common and scientific names.
- Geographic distribution and elevation range.
- Habitat and feeding ecology.
- Nesting behaviour and breeding season.
- Migration or seasonal movements.
- Conservation status and known threats.
- Similar species and distinguishing field marks.
- Sources, references, and date of review.

This structure will let readers compare species across mountain ranges without confusing regional differences.

## Flight, seasons and the mountain atmosphere

Air currents are a defining part of mountain bird ecology. Rising air along sun-warmed slopes and ridges can help soaring birds gain height with less flapping, while exposed ridges can also bring powerful turbulence and sudden weather changes. Small birds may use sheltered vegetation, rock crevices or dense shrubs to reduce wind exposure. The same terrain that creates opportunities for flight can also make movement difficult when cloud, storms or strong winds arrive.

Birds use different parts of the mountain system. Forest songbirds depend on canopy structure, nesting sites and seasonal food. Ground-dwelling pheasants and partridges use scrub, grassland or forest floor. Stream specialists forage in running water, while large scavengers rely on carcasses and suitable nesting cliffs. A list of birds therefore becomes more informative when each species is connected to its habitat and feeding strategy.

## Migration, breeding and long-term monitoring

The presence of a bird changes with season. Some species move along elevation gradients as food and weather shift; others migrate over long distances. Breeding habitat can be more restrictive than foraging habitat, and a bird that is seen passing over a summit may not use that summit for nesting. Surveys should record the date, weather, observation effort and habitat, especially when results are compared between years.

Birds can also be useful indicators of landscape change, but they do not provide a single universal measure of ecosystem health. Changes in abundance can reflect weather, migration, observer effort, food availability or habitat loss. Repeated observations and careful identification are more reliable than a single sighting. Ethical birdwatching keeps distance from nests and avoids playback or flash when these might stress birds.
