Trees influence the physical environment as well as supporting wildlife. Their roots stabilise soils, their canopies modify temperature and light, and their leaves and litter contribute to nutrient cycling.

## 7.1 Coniferous forests

Conifers are important in many cool mountain environments. Their needle-like leaves, growth forms, and reproductive structures vary between species, and many are adapted to cold winters or relatively short growing seasons.

Examples include:

- Norway spruce (Picea abies).
- Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii).
- Himalayan blue pine (Pinus wallichiana).
- Swiss stone pine (Pinus cembra).
- European larch (Larix decidua).
- Atlas cedar (Cedrus atlantica).
- Subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa).

Conifer forests provide nesting sites, food, shelter, and habitat continuity for many birds and mammals. Their ecological role also depends on disturbance, soil conditions, and whether forests are naturally mixed or dominated by one species.

## 7.2 Broadleaf mountain forests

Broadleaf forests are particularly important in many temperate and subtropical mountain regions. They support diverse invertebrate communities, fruit and seed resources, and seasonal changes in light availability.

Examples include:

- European beech (Fagus sylvatica).
- American beech (Fagus grandifolia).
- Japanese beech (Fagus crenata).
- Himalayan oaks, including species in the genus Quercus.
- Various maples (Acer species).
- Birches (Betula species).

Different trees provide different food resources and nesting structures. Forest diversity can therefore influence the variety of birds, mammals, insects, and fungi present.

Near the tree line, trees may grow slowly and become short, twisted, or shrub-like. These forms can result from wind exposure, snow pressure, frost damage, and limited growing-season length.

Some mountain pines form low mats that remain close to the ground, while certain deciduous shrubs survive in exposed locations where taller trees cannot establish.

The tree line is valuable for research because its position and structure reflect several environmental variables. Changes over time can reveal the combined effects of climate, disturbance, and land use.

## 7.4 Alpine wildflowers

Alpine plants have evolved a wide variety of strategies for surviving cold, wind, intense sunlight, and short growing seasons.

Examples include edelweiss in European mountains, Himalayan blue poppies in suitable Himalayan habitats, alpine gentians, primulas, mountain avens, and cushion-forming plants.

Many alpine flowers depend on insects for pollination. The timing of flowering and insect activity can be important for successful reproduction.

Collecting rare plants, trampling sensitive meadows, and introducing non-native species can damage these communities. Botanical observation should prioritise conservation and follow local access rules.

## Why the tree line is not a simple altitude number

The climatic tree line reflects the combined effects of temperature, length of growing season, wind, snow cover and soil conditions. Its position changes between latitude, mountains and even opposite sides of a single peak. Trees on an exposed ridge may be stunted or absent where a sheltered slope at the same elevation supports woodland. Fire, grazing and human land use can also create an apparent tree-line boundary that is not controlled by climate alone.

Above the tree line, plants use different strategies. Low-growing cushions and mats can reduce exposure to wind; hairs and compact growth may help buffer temperature; and seasonal flowering allows reproduction during a short period when conditions are favourable. Lichens can colonise rock surfaces where soil is scarce. These communities may be resilient to harsh weather yet vulnerable to trampling because cold environments and short growing seasons slow recovery.

## Forests shape water and microclimate

Tree canopies shade streams, intercept rainfall, add organic matter and provide structure for birds, insects and mammals. Roots can stabilise some soils, although forests cannot prevent every landslide or flood. The effect depends on geology, slope, the intensity of rainfall and the condition of the vegetation. When forest composition changes through fire, pests, logging, invasive species or climate shifts, the impacts can extend to water, soils and food webs.

When identifying mountain plants, record leaf shape, flower structure, growth form, elevation, substrate and nearby species. Photograph multiple features and use a regional field guide. Avoid collecting plants in protected places, and treat a plant identification as provisional if the most important features are not visible.
