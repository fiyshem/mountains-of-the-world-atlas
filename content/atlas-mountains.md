The Atlas Mountains extend across northwestern Africa. Their environments range from Mediterranean woodland and montane forests to rocky highlands and semi-arid slopes near the Sahara.

The Atlas Mountains demonstrate how elevation can create cooler and wetter habitats within a generally warm region. Their ecology is strongly influenced by seasonal rainfall, snow at higher elevations, drought, grazing, and human land use.

## Mammals

- Barbary macaque (Macaca sylvanus): a primate associated with woodland habitats, especially cedar and oak forests in parts of Morocco and Algeria.
- Barbary sheep or aoudad (Ammotragus lervia): a wild caprine adapted to rugged, dry terrain.
- North African wild boar (Sus scrofa populations): found in suitable woodland and scrub habitats.
- Red fox (Vulpes vulpes): an adaptable carnivore occurring across a broad range of environments.
- Cuvier's gazelle (Gazella cuvieri): a North African antelope associated with suitable mountainous and open habitats.

## Birds

- Levaillant's woodpecker (Picus vaillantii): a North African woodpecker associated with forested habitats.
- Moussier's redstart (Phoenicurus moussieri): a small songbird of northwestern Africa, including shrubland and open woodland.
- Barbary partridge (Alectoris barbara): a ground-dwelling bird of rocky, scrubby, and open habitats.
- Bonelli's eagle (Aquila fasciata): a raptor that hunts in open and rugged landscapes.
- Common raven (Corvus corax): an adaptable bird found in a wide range of mountain environments.

## Trees and plants

- Atlas cedar (Cedrus atlantica): an important montane conifer of parts of the Atlas region.
- Holm oak (Quercus ilex): an evergreen oak adapted to Mediterranean climates.
- Barbary thuya (Tetraclinis articulata): a drought-tolerant conifer-like tree of northwestern Africa.
- Junipers: important shrubs and trees in many dry mountain habitats.
- Aromatic herbs and drought-adapted shrubs: plants that tolerate seasonal water scarcity and exposed terrain.

The Atlas Mountains are valuable for studying the boundary between Mediterranean and Saharan environments. Drought, erosion, fire, overgrazing in some areas, and forest degradation can threaten the stability of mountain habitats.

## Water in a seasonally dry mountain region

The Atlas shows how mountains can create cooler and wetter environments inside a broader region with strong seasonal water limits. Higher elevations may receive winter snow or more rainfall, while exposed slopes and the foothills toward arid country can be much drier. Springs, snowmelt and mountain streams are therefore important to downstream settlements, agriculture and natural habitats. Their flow can vary sharply between seasons and years.

Forests of Atlas cedar, holm oak and other native vegetation help shape soil, shade and habitat where local conditions support them. Woodland condition varies with elevation, rainfall, fire, grazing and land use. Where vegetation cover is reduced on steep ground, heavy rain can carry sediment downslope and contribute to erosion or damage to cultivated land.

## Landscapes shaped by people and climate

The Atlas should not be viewed only as a wilderness boundary beside the Sahara. It contains inhabited valleys, grazing areas, farming landscapes and cultural routes, and the livelihoods of communities depend on local conditions and long-standing knowledge. Conservation strategies work best when they account for these needs alongside woodland regeneration, soil protection and wildlife habitat.

A field study here might compare a shaded forested slope with a sun-exposed rocky slope. Record differences in plant cover, soil moisture, grazing signs and runoff pathways. Then investigate how rainfall seasonality, aspect and human use combine to explain the contrast. This approach also helps explain why a regional response to drought or fire should be based on local evidence rather than one rule for the whole range.
