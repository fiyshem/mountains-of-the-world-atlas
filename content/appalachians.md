The Appalachian Mountains extend from eastern Canada through the eastern United States. They are an ancient mountain system whose present rounded ridges and deep valleys have been shaped by prolonged erosion.

The Appalachians support temperate deciduous forests, mixed coniferous forests, wetlands, rocky outcrops, and high-elevation spruce–fir communities. Their long geological history and varied climate have contributed to substantial regional biodiversity, including numerous salamanders and locally restricted species.&#x20;

## Mammals

- American black bear (Ursus americanus): an omnivore that uses forests, clearings, and other habitats.
- White-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus): a widespread herbivore whose browsing influences vegetation.
- Bobcat (Lynx rufus): a medium-sized predator of forests and mixed landscapes.
- North American river otter (Lontra canadensis): a semi-aquatic mammal associated with healthy waterways.
- Eastern chipmunk (Tamias striatus): a small mammal that stores seeds and other food.
- Eastern coyote (Canis latrans populations): a flexible predator and scavenger found in many Appalachian landscapes.

## Birds

- Pileated woodpecker (Dryocopus pileatus): excavates large cavities in trees and feeds on insects, including ants.
- Wood thrush (Hylocichla mustelina): a forest songbird that nests in woodland and feeds on invertebrates and fruit.
- Scarlet tanager (Piranga olivacea): a brightly coloured migratory songbird of mature deciduous forests.
- Ruffed grouse (Bonasa umbellus): a forest grouse associated with woodland understory and regenerating forest.
- Cerulean warbler (Setophaga cerulea): a migratory bird associated with mature deciduous forests.
- Wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo): a large ground-foraging bird of woodland and open habitats.

## Trees and plants

- Eastern hemlock (Tsuga canadensis): a shade-tolerant conifer important to cool, moist forest environments.
- Red spruce (Picea rubens): a conifer associated with cooler high-elevation habitats.
- American beech (Fagus grandifolia): a deciduous tree that produces nuts consumed by wildlife.
- Sugar maple (Acer saccharum): a broadleaf tree characteristic of many northern and upland forests.
- Tulip tree (Liriodendron tulipifera): a tall hardwood of eastern North American forests.
- Mountain laurel (Kalmia latifolia): an evergreen shrub of acidic soils and woodland understories.

This range is especially valuable for studying forest succession, freshwater ecosystems, salamander diversity, and the ecological effects of invasive pests. Hemlock woolly adelgid, habitat fragmentation, acid deposition in some areas, and climate change have affected parts of the Appalachian landscape.

## Ancient rocks, living streams

Although the Appalachians look gentler than many young high mountain ranges, their rounded ridges contain complex geological structures left by ancient mountain-building events. Long periods of weathering and erosion have lowered the relief, while rivers have cut valleys through resistant and less resistant rocks. Rock age, mineral composition and the shape of the land still influence soils, stream channels and the distribution of habitats.

The region’s humid temperate climate supports a broad mixture of deciduous and evergreen forests. Sheltered ravines, cool headwater streams, rocky outcrops and high-elevation spruce–fir communities create many small habitat differences. Amphibians, including highly diverse salamander communities in parts of the range, are particularly useful for showing how moisture, cool microclimates and barriers between valleys can affect distribution.

## Forest continuity and practical conservation

A connected forest can support movement, breeding and recovery after disturbance. Roads, development, invasive pests and changes in climate can fragment or alter forest habitat. In some locations, pests such as hemlock woolly adelgid affect a tree species that contributes to cool, moist conditions in ravines and along streams. The ecological consequences can extend beyond the tree itself.

For a field comparison, examine a shaded stream hollow and a drier ridge top. Look for differences in leaf litter, moss, tree species, temperature and moisture, then consider how these local conditions support different organisms. Small landscape features matter here: not every important ecological transition is visible from the distant skyline.
