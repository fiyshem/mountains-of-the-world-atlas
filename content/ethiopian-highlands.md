The Ethiopian Highlands and the mountains of East Africa contain distinctive tropical high-altitude environments. Unlike the Alps or Himalayas, some of these landscapes occur within the tropics, where strong elevation gradients create cool montane forests and alpine habitats surrounded by warmer lowlands.

The region includes the Simien Mountains, Bale Mountains, and other highland systems. Its wildlife includes several highly specialised species, some of which have exceptionally restricted ranges.

## Mammals

- Gelada (Theropithecus gelada): a grass-eating primate native to the Ethiopian Highlands, often found on high plateaus and steep escarpments.
- Walia ibex (Capra walie): a rare wild goat associated with rugged cliffs in the Simien Mountains.
- Ethiopian wolf (Canis simensis): a specialised carnivore of high-altitude Ethiopian grasslands and heathlands.
- Mountain nyala (Tragelaphus buxtoni): a large antelope associated with Ethiopian montane habitats.
- Giant mole-rats (Tachyoryctes species): subterranean mammals that play roles in soil processes and are important prey for certain predators.

## Birds

- Thick-billed raven (Corvus crassirostris): a large corvid native to the Horn of Africa.
- Wattled ibis (Bostrychia carunculata): a bird associated with highland habitats and open landscapes.
- Blue-winged goose (Cyanochen cyanoptera): an endemic waterfowl species of Ethiopian highlands.
- Rouget's rail (Rougetius rougetii): a bird of highland streams, marshes, and grassy environments.
- Lammergeier or bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus): occurs in some mountainous regions across a much wider range.

## Plants

- Giant lobelia (Lobelia rhynchopetalum): a striking high-altitude plant of the Ethiopian mountains.
- Giant heather (Erica arborea): a woody species that can contribute to montane heathland and woodland.
- Afromontane junipers (Juniperus procera): conifers found in parts of African montane forests.
- Afroalpine grasses and herbs: plants adapted to large daily temperature fluctuations and strong solar radiation.

These mountain systems illustrate tropical alpine ecology, where freezing nights can occur despite a tropical latitude. Habitat conversion, grazing, agricultural expansion, and climate change are important concerns for specialised highland species.

## Tropical latitude, alpine conditions

The Ethiopian Highlands demonstrate that cold, high-elevation environments are not restricted to temperate latitudes. Strong daily temperature swings, intense sunlight and a short or variable growing season shape Afroalpine vegetation. Giant lobelias and giant heathers are striking examples of plants adapted to these conditions, while highland grasslands provide habitat for mammals such as geladas and Ethiopian wolves.

Isolation among highland plateaus and escarpments can help produce local endemism. At the same time, the amount and quality of natural habitat available to a species may be limited by the presence of farms, settlements or heavily grazed land. Protecting a summit or cliff alone may not be sufficient if the species also relies on grassland, water or movement routes around it.

## High-water landscapes and community needs

Highlands can collect and distribute water to lower areas, linking upland rainfall and springs with agriculture, settlements and river systems far downstream. Erosion, vegetation loss, grazing and land conversion can affect soil and runoff. The best way to understand these processes is to combine observations at the slope scale with watershed-level information and the knowledge of people who use the landscape.

A study of an Afroalpine site could record the height and density of vegetation, exposure, evidence of grazing, wet ground and the relationship between wildlife habitat and nearby land use. Because species ranges may be narrow and conservation status can change, current regional sources should be checked before making a claim about abundance or threat level.
