# The Cascade Range

Learn how a volcanic arc, wet forests, snowpack and river headwaters shape the landscapes of western North America.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## A volcanic arc

The Cascade Range extends through western North America and includes many volcanic peaks formed in a tectonic setting associated with subduction along the Pacific margin. Volcanoes differ in age, structure and current activity; not every peak is equally active or carries the same hazards. Volcanic rocks, glacial carving and river erosion combine to produce steep ridges, cones, plateaus and broad valleys.

## Rain, snow and rain shadows

Moist Pacific air rises over the western slopes, supporting substantial precipitation and productive forests in many areas. Descending air east of the crest can be drier, creating strong ecological contrasts over relatively short distances. Snowpack stores winter precipitation and releases water during warmer months, while warming trends are changing snow duration and the seasonal timing of runoff in many basins.

## Forests and disturbance

Conifer forests include species adapted to different elevations, soils, fire regimes and moisture conditions. Fire, insects, windthrow and volcanic disturbance have helped shape these forests, although the ecological effects vary. Forest management, roads, recreation and development can affect habitat connectivity and water quality.

## Living near volcanoes

Some communities and infrastructure lie within reach of ash, lahars, landslides or other volcanic hazards. Monitoring, hazard maps, public communication and evacuation planning reduce risk but cannot remove it. USGS volcanic hazard materials provide a starting point for understanding monitoring and emergency planning.
