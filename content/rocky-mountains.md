The Rocky Mountains extend through western Canada and the United States. Their landscapes include high alpine peaks, coniferous forests, meadows, glacial lakes, river valleys, and dry eastern foothills.

The Rockies have a complex geological history involving ancient crustal processes, periods of compression, uplift, faulting, volcanism in some areas, and extensive erosion. Their exact geological origins differ between sections of the range.

## Mammals

- American black bear (Ursus americanus): an adaptable omnivore that feeds on plants, insects, and animal foods.
- Grizzly bear (Ursus arctos): a large omnivore that can influence ecosystems through predation, scavenging, and movement of nutrients.
- Rocky Mountain elk (Cervus canadensis): a large herbivore whose grazing and browsing can influence forest regeneration and meadow vegetation.
- Bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis): an agile ungulate associated with rugged, rocky terrain.
- Mountain goat (Oreamnos americanus): adapted to steep slopes, cold environments, and exposed alpine habitat.
- American pika (Ochotona princeps): a small mountain-dwelling mammal that gathers vegetation for winter food and can be sensitive to warming conditions.

## Birds

- Clark's nutcracker (Nucifraga columbiana): a corvid that caches pine seeds, helping disperse certain conifers.
- Mountain bluebird (Sialia currucoides): a cavity-nesting bird of open mountain habitats and grasslands.
- White-tailed ptarmigan (Lagopus leucura): an alpine grouse with seasonal plumage changes that help camouflage it in different conditions.
- Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos): a raptor that uses open slopes and updrafts to hunt and travel.
- Steller's jay (Cyanocitta stelleri): an adaptable corvid of western forests.
- American dipper (Cinclus mexicanus): a stream-associated bird capable of foraging underwater in cold, flowing water.

## Trees and plants

- Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii): a high-elevation conifer that often forms subalpine forests.
- Subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa): an evergreen tree adapted to cool mountain environments.
- Lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta): a common western conifer whose regeneration in some landscapes is strongly influenced by fire.
- Quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides): a deciduous tree that can form extensive stands and regenerate through root suckers.
- Alpine avens and other alpine wildflowers: flowering plants that exploit the short growing season above or near the tree line.
- Sagebrush: a dominant plant of many drier foothills and intermountain landscapes, supporting specialised wildlife.

The Rockies are important for watershed protection, wildlife corridors, forest ecology, and the study of fire and climate. Their ecosystems are affected by drought, invasive species, road development, altered fire regimes, and changes in snowpack.&#x20;

## A range of climates in one mountain system

The Rockies are best understood as a sequence of habitats rather than a single forest. Elevation and slope exposure separate valley bottoms, montane woods, subalpine forests, open alpine terrain and dry foothills. Different parts of the range experience different precipitation regimes, and the eastern side can transition toward grasslands and shrublands while wetter slopes support forests. These contrasts help explain why regional wildlife communities differ even within the same broad mountain chain.

Snowpack, springs and small headwater streams connect the high landscape to larger river systems. Forest condition affects shade, soil stability, habitat and the way water moves across a watershed. Fire is also a natural ecological process in many western forests, although the ecological role of fire differs among forest types and can be altered by drought, land management and past suppression.

## Reading wildlife signs responsibly

Large mammals such as elk, bighorn sheep, mountain goats and bears are charismatic, but smaller species can be equally revealing. Pikas depend on suitable rocky habitat and gather vegetation for winter food; Clark's nutcrackers cache pine seeds and can help regenerate certain conifers. Stream birds such as American dippers indicate a very different habitat from meadow-nesting or forest birds. The species present at a site reflect food, shelter, season and human disturbance, not just altitude.

When studying the Rockies, compare a high alpine basin with a lower forest edge. Note snow cover, aspect, tree regeneration, stream condition and evidence of animal movement. Conservation questions often involve road networks, recreation pressure, drought, invasive species, fire and the preservation of habitat corridors that allow wildlife to move across large landscapes.
