# Fungi, Lichens, Mosses & Hidden Mountain Life

Look beneath the canopy at fungal partnerships, lichen symbiosis, mosses and microbes that build soil and sustain mountain ecosystems.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Fungi connect roots and soils

Fungi decompose dead wood, leaves and other organic matter, returning nutrients to ecosystems. Many form **mycorrhizal associations** with plant roots: fungal networks can help plants acquire nutrients and water, while receiving carbon compounds made through photosynthesis. These partnerships vary among plant and fungal groups and are influenced by soil, moisture, disturbance and season. The underground network is not one single organism or a universal cooperative system; its interactions can be complex.

## Lichens are partnerships

A lichen is a stable association involving a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, usually an alga or cyanobacterium. Lichens can colonise rock, bark, soil and exposed branches, including cold and nutrient-poor sites. They contribute to weathering, provide microhabitats and can serve as indicators of particular environmental conditions. Because lichens grow slowly in many settings, trampling, collecting or air pollution may leave a long-lasting mark.

## Mosses and liverworts hold tiny worlds

Bryophytes lack the complex vascular systems of trees and many flowering plants, yet they can hold water, create humid microsites and trap fine particles. A moss mat may shelter tiny invertebrates and influence seedling establishment. Species respond differently to moisture, light and exposure. Their presence can reveal seepage or persistent dampness, but field identification may require close inspection and regional keys.

## Microbes drive unseen cycles

Bacteria, archaea and microscopic fungi help transform carbon, nitrogen and other elements. Communities in snow, springs, soils and rock surfaces differ with temperature, chemistry and available nutrients. Scientists study these communities using microscopy, DNA-based tools and controlled experiments. A photo alone rarely identifies a microbe, and claims about its function need evidence beyond its presence.

## Observe without damaging the habitat

Use a hand lens, close-focus photographs and notes on substrate, moisture, shade and elevation. Do not scrape lichens from old trees or rocks for a souvenir, and avoid stepping on fragile mats where a trail can be used instead. Record the scale in photographs and compare observations with a trusted regional guide. Small organisms often provide clues about environmental conditions that are invisible at first glance.
