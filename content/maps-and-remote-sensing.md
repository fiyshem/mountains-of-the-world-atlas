# Mountain Maps, GIS & Remote Sensing

Use contour maps, digital elevation models, GPS and satellite imagery to study terrain while understanding scale, resolution and uncertainty.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Start with scale and contours

A topographic map represents elevation with contour lines: each line joins points of equal height. Closely spaced contours usually indicate a steep slope; widely spaced contours indicate gentler terrain. Closed contours, hachures, symbols, shaded relief and labelled spot heights add context. Map scale determines how much detail can be represented. A map that is excellent for regional planning may be inadequate for choosing a safe foot placement or identifying a small stream.

## Coordinate systems and position

Latitude and longitude describe angular position, while projected coordinate systems represent locations on a flat grid. Different coordinate reference systems can produce small or large alignment errors when mixed. GPS readings also have uncertainty caused by satellite geometry, canopy, cliffs, atmosphere and device quality. Record the coordinate system, date and estimated accuracy when location precision matters.

## Digital elevation models and terrain derivatives

A digital elevation model (DEM) stores elevation values in a grid or other structured representation. From it, analysts can derive slope, aspect, curvature, watershed boundaries and viewshed estimates. Results depend on resolution, data quality, interpolation, vegetation treatment and the algorithm used. A coarse DEM may smooth narrow ridges or miss small gullies; a detailed DEM can still contain artefacts. Model outputs should not be treated as ground truth without validation.

## Satellite and aerial imagery

Remote sensing can reveal snow cover, glacier area, forest change, burned areas, landslides and land-use patterns across difficult terrain. Different sensors detect visible light, infrared energy, radar returns or elevation. Clouds, shadows, snow, steep viewing angles and seasonal timing can affect interpretation. A colour composite is a processed display, not necessarily what the landscape looked like to a person standing there.

## Build a reproducible map

Record the source, acquisition date, licence, scale or resolution, coordinate system, processing steps and limits. Compare imagery from similar seasons where possible. Verify key features with field observations or independent datasets. Do not publish exact locations of rare species, vulnerable archaeological sites or sensitive cultural places without an appropriate reason and permission.

## Questions to carry into the field

- What direct evidence supports this interpretation, and what remains an assumption?
- Which local conditions, seasons or communities would change the answer?
- What source or repeated observation could help verify the explanation?
