## 1.1 What is a mountain?

A mountain is a substantial natural elevation of Earth's surface, rising above its surrounding terrain. There is no universally accepted elevation threshold that separates every mountain from every hill.

Mountains may occur individually, in groups, or as parts of enormous interconnected ranges. Some are formed by the collision of tectonic plates; others develop through volcanic activity, faulting, uplift, or long periods of erosion.

Their shapes vary according to the type of rock, geological history, climate, and the processes acting on their surfaces. Sharp, jagged peaks often reflect resistant rock and glacial erosion, while older mountain systems may have rounded summits and deeply weathered slopes.

## 1.2 The principal types of mountains

Fold mountains

Form when enormous compressional forces deform and uplift rock layers. The Himalayas and Alps are major examples associated with the collision of continental tectonic plates.

Key process: compression, folding, and crustal thickening.

Volcanic mountains

Develop when magma reaches the surface and erupts as lava, ash, and other volcanic material. Their shapes depend on the composition and viscosity of magma and the style of eruption.

Examples: Mount Fuji, Mount Etna, and Mount Kilimanjaro.

Fault-block mountains

Develop when movement along geological faults lifts, tilts, or separates large blocks of Earth's crust.

Example: the Teton Range in Wyoming, USA.

Eroded and ancient mountain systems

Long periods of weathering, river erosion, and other surface processes can lower and reshape once-tall mountain belts. Some old mountain systems retain complex geological structures despite their rounded appearance.

Examples: parts of the Appalachians and Scottish Highlands.

## 1.3 How mountains are formed

Earth's outer shell is divided into tectonic plates that move over geological time. Their interactions produce many of the world's major mountain systems.

When two continental plates converge, the crust may buckle, thicken, and rise. When an oceanic plate descends beneath another plate, melting and magma formation can create volcanic mountain chains. Fault movements can elevate blocks of crust, while uplift and erosion continually reshape the landscape.

Mountain building is not a single event. It can involve repeated episodes of deformation, intrusion, metamorphism, uplift, erosion, and renewed tectonic activity over millions of years.

## 1.4 Why mountains are still changing

Mountains are dynamic landscapes. Even apparently solid peaks are modified by physical, chemical, and biological processes.

- Weathering: rock breaks down through temperature changes, water, chemical reactions, and biological activity.
- Erosion: rivers, glaciers, wind, and gravity remove and transport material.
- Landslides: unstable slopes fail when gravitational forces overcome the strength of rock or soil.
- Glacial erosion: moving ice abrades and plucks rock, producing valleys and cirques.
- Tectonic uplift: crustal movements raise landscapes and renew relief.
- Sedimentation: eroded material accumulates in valleys, river plains, lakes, and offshore basins.

The landscape that exists today reflects the combined effects of these processes acting at different speeds.

## A mountain-building timeline

It is useful to separate the timescale that builds a range from the timescale that reshapes its surface. Tectonic convergence, fault displacement and crustal uplift may operate over millions of years, while a storm, freeze–thaw cycle, rockfall or landslide can alter a particular slope much faster. Rivers continually carry sediment away from uplands, and glaciers can erode and transport enormous quantities of rock when conditions allow them to persist.

The processes also interact. Uplift creates relief that can increase erosion; erosion removes weight from the crust and may influence later uplift; rock type helps determine how slopes weather; and climate determines whether water flows as rain, seasonal snowmelt or moving ice. The present shape of a mountain is the outcome of all these forces rather than the product of one event.

## How to read a mountain from the outside

Start with the broad form. A long chain of aligned ridges may reflect regional tectonic structure, while a cone-shaped summit may suggest volcanic origin. A broad U-shaped valley may record glacial erosion, whereas a narrow V-shaped valley often reflects river incision. These are clues, not automatic proofs: landforms can have several stages of history, and later erosion can obscure earlier features.

Next, look at the rock and drainage. Exposed layers, joints, faults, landslide scars, alluvial fans and river terraces reveal how rock and sediment move through the landscape. A reliable geological interpretation combines several observations with maps, field descriptions and regional evidence. A photograph is useful, but it rarely reveals the entire history by itself.
