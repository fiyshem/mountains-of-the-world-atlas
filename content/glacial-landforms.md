# Glaciers & Glacial Landforms

Read cirques, horns, arêtes, U-shaped valleys and moraines as evidence of moving ice and changing mountain climates.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## How mountain glaciers form and flow

A glacier develops where snow accumulation persists over time and compacts into firn and then ice. Under its own weight, glacier ice deforms and may slide over the ground. Its mass balance reflects the difference between accumulation and ablation, which includes melting, sublimation and calving where ice reaches water. A glacier can advance or retreat depending on its mass balance and dynamics; its terminus may respond slowly to climate because ice flow takes time to adjust.

## Erosional landforms

Glaciers can abrade bedrock with embedded debris and pluck fractured rock from valley walls. A bowl-shaped **cirque** often marks a glacier source area. Sharp **arêtes** can form between adjacent glacial basins, while a pointed **horn** may develop where several cirques erode toward one summit. Large valley glaciers often deepen and widen valleys into U-shaped cross-sections, leaving steep sides and broad floors. Hanging valleys and over-steepened steps can influence waterfalls and lake basins.

## Depositional landforms

When ice transports and releases debris, it leaves unsorted sediment called till. Ridges of this material can form moraines at glacier margins or along former flow paths. Meltwater sorts sand and gravel into outwash deposits, and streams may build braided channels across valley floors. Erratic boulders, polished bedrock and striations can preserve evidence of past ice movement. These features may persist long after a glacier disappears, but later rivers and slope processes continue to modify them.

## Landforms as climate archives

Glacial landforms can reveal the extent and direction of former ice, helping researchers reconstruct past environments. Dating methods, sediment cores, historical photographs and satellite observations add timescales to the visible landscape. Not every lake or U-shaped valley is exclusively glacial in origin, and different processes can overlap. Good interpretation compares landform shape, sediment, elevation, geological structure and regional climate history.

## Why present-day change matters

Shrinking ice can alter seasonal runoff, expose unstable slopes and contribute to the formation or expansion of proglacial lakes. Meltwater availability can shift across seasons, affecting hydropower, irrigation, ecosystems and communities. Glacier change is only one component of mountain water systems; rain, groundwater, seasonal snow and reservoirs also shape river flow. Avoid treating a single glacier photograph as a complete measure of a region’s water security.
