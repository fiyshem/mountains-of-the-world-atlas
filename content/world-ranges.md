A mountain range is not simply a row of peaks. It is a geographic system: ridges and valleys organise water, elevation shapes temperature and vegetation, and geological history influences soils, landforms and natural hazards. Mountain ranges can extend across national borders and contain several ecological regions at once.

The atlas compares major mountain systems through the same lens: **where a range lies, how its rocks and landforms developed, what climate patterns it influences, which habitats it supports, how people use the landscape, and what pressures may affect its future.** Use the regional pages as starting points rather than uniform descriptions of every valley in a range.

| Himalayas | South and Central Asia | Active continental collision, very high peaks, glaciers, deep valleys and strong elevation gradients |
| Andes | Western South America | Subduction-related uplift and volcanism, high plateaus, puna grasslands, cloud forests and glaciers |
| Rocky Mountains | Western Canada and the United States | Alpine basins, conifer forests, dry foothills, snow-fed headwaters and large mammals |
| Alps | Central and southern Europe | Glacial valleys, limestone terrain, forests, high pastures and densely connected human landscapes |
| Caucasus | Between the Black Sea and Caspian Sea | High relief, glaciers, mixed biogeographic influences and local endemism |

## Why mountain ranges differ

**Tectonic setting** influences the shapes and materials of mountains. Continental collision can fold and thicken crust; subduction can create volcanic arcs and uplift; fault movement can raise blocks of rock. The resulting landforms are then reshaped by rivers, glaciers, frost, wind and gravity. A single range may contain rocks of different ages and origins, so one explanation rarely applies to every peak.

**Climate and location** shape the living landscape. Mountains near the equator may have cool highlands above tropical forest. Maritime ranges exposed to moist air can receive abundant rain and snow, while ranges downwind of a barrier may contain dry grasslands or cold deserts. Latitude, aspect, prevailing winds and distance from the ocean all matter alongside elevation.

**Geographic isolation** creates opportunities for evolution. Ridges and valleys can divide habitats; isolated high plateaus or summit environments can support species found nowhere else. At the same time, rivers, passes and forest corridors connect populations and help species move between habitats. Neither isolation nor connectivity is uniformly good or bad: the outcome depends on a species' life history and changing conditions.

## How to read a regional page

Begin with the broad location, then look for the physical processes that make the range distinctive. Follow the ecological links: which forest belts occur at lower elevations, what replaces forest near the tree line, which animals depend on alpine meadows, and how streams connect the upper slopes to communities downstream. Finally, consider human presence, conservation priorities and the kinds of evidence needed to understand local change.

## Beyond the best-known ranges

The great mountain story continues through the Alaska Range, Sierra Nevada, Cascades, Sierra Madre, Rwenzori Mountains, Drakensberg, Great Dividing Range, Sudetes, Carpathians, Tien Shan, Pamirs, Hindu Kush, Zagros and Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. Other mountain regions include island arcs, ancient highlands, volcanic cones and isolated tropical massifs. The additional-systems page introduces many of these landscapes and the ecological questions they raise.

Mountain names, regional boundaries and summit measurements can vary according to definition and source. When exact data matter, compare regional geological surveys, official protected-area information and current scientific references rather than relying on a broad atlas description alone.
