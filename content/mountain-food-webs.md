# Mountain Food Webs & Ecological Relationships

Connect plants, herbivores, predators, scavengers and decomposers to understand the flow of energy through mountain habitats.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## From sunlight to living tissue

Most terrestrial food webs begin with producers such as green plants and algae that capture energy through photosynthesis. Herbivores feed on plant material; predators eat other animals; scavengers use carcasses; decomposers process dead material and waste. Real food webs are networks rather than straight lines: one species may use several food sources and be eaten by multiple predators across seasons.

## A species has more than one role

An animal’s ecological role can change with age, season, habitat or food availability. Bears, for example, may consume berries, roots, insects and animal matter; raptors can prey on mammals and birds; and birds may disperse seeds while consuming fruit. Large herbivores can influence vegetation through grazing and browsing, but their effect depends on density, plant communities, soil and predator relationships. Avoid assuming that a species performs the same role in every mountain range.

## Predators and cascading effects

Predators can affect prey numbers and behaviour, which may influence vegetation or the use of open spaces. Such **trophic cascades** are context-dependent: climate, hunting, disease, human land use and habitat structure may strengthen or weaken them. A change observed after predator recovery does not automatically prove that the predator caused every ecological difference. Good studies compare time periods, control sites and several interacting variables.

## Nutrient pathways and seasonal bottlenecks

Carcasses, dung, leaf litter and dead wood move nutrients between parts of a landscape. Rivers carry organic material from headwaters downstream, while migratory animals may connect elevations or distant regions. Winter can reduce accessible food, and deep snow may change what herbivores can reach. A short flowering period can concentrate insects and birds in a small area, creating temporary but important ecological links.

## Building a simple food-web sketch

Choose a specific habitat and season. Start with a handful of verified species, connect only relationships supported by observation or a reliable source, and use arrows to show the direction of energy from food to consumer. Mark uncertain links with a question mark. Then ask what would happen if a resource declined or a connection were severed. This is a learning model, not a complete representation of an ecosystem.
