# Human Physiology, Altitude & Acclimatisation

Understand why oxygen availability declines with elevation and why fitness alone does not prevent altitude illness.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Why the air feels different

As elevation rises, atmospheric pressure falls, so each breath delivers less oxygen pressure to the lungs even though oxygen remains about the same proportion of dry air. The body responds by increasing breathing and making other short- and longer-term adjustments. The pace and extent of adaptation vary with the individual, ascent profile, sleep, exertion, hydration and existing health conditions.

## Acclimatisation takes time

Gradual ascent gives the body time to adapt. Rapidly gaining sleeping elevation can increase the risk of acute mountain sickness and more serious altitude-related illness. Being physically fit does not eliminate the risk, and a person’s previous response does not guarantee what will happen on another trip. Symptoms should not be ignored or masked simply to continue a planned itinerary.

## Warning signs and decisions

Headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue and poor appetite can occur with acute mountain sickness, but symptoms vary and other illnesses can look similar. Confusion, difficulty walking straight, breathlessness at rest or worsening symptoms can signal a medical emergency. Descent and urgent medical assessment may be necessary. This article is educational and cannot diagnose an individual or replace professional advice.

## Plan for the whole group

Plan sleeping elevations and rest days conservatively, communicate symptoms openly, and make it acceptable for any team member to stop or descend. Know the emergency options and local rescue limitations before departure. Avoid using a generic altitude schedule as a substitute for recognised medical guidance tailored to the route and people travelling.
