# Mountain Agriculture, Pastoralism & Food Systems

Explore terraces, seasonal grazing, agroforestry, local crop diversity and the adaptations that help communities farm steep terrain.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Farming across elevation bands

Mountain farms may be distributed across small plots at different elevations and aspects. Temperature, frost, slope, soil depth, rainfall and access determine which crops and livestock systems are feasible. Families may combine cereals, tubers, fruit trees, vegetables, grazing, forests and non-farm income. This diversity can spread risk, but steep land, limited mechanisation, transport costs and unpredictable weather create significant constraints.

## Terraces, irrigation and shared infrastructure

Terraces reduce slope length and can create cultivable surfaces, but their stability depends on construction, drainage and maintenance. Canals, springs, storage ponds and community water rules may connect fields separated by steep terrain. Poorly drained terraces can concentrate water and fail during storms. Traditional methods can embody generations of local observation; they should be understood in context rather than romanticised as automatically sustainable in every modern condition.

## Pastoralism and agroforestry

Seasonal movement of livestock allows herds to use changing forage resources and can reduce pressure on one pasture when managed well. Grazing outcomes depend on stocking density, timing, plant recovery, predators, water access and customary rights. Agroforestry combines trees with crops or livestock, potentially providing shade, fodder, fuel, soil protection and habitat. Species choices must fit local climate, biodiversity and water availability.

## Food security and local economies

Mountain food systems often depend on access to markets, storage, roads, healthcare, education and reliable water. Climate shifts can change planting windows and pest pressure; migration can alter labour availability; tourism may create new markets as well as seasonal dependence. Support for local processing, seed diversity, fair market access and infrastructure designed for slope conditions can strengthen resilience when communities shape the decisions.

## A respectful learning approach

Do not describe a community as a single uniform culture. Farming systems vary within a valley and change across generations. When visiting agricultural landscapes, ask permission before photographing people, avoid walking through crops, respect water and grazing rules, and recognise local expertise. FAO’s Mountain Partnership resources provide further reading on sustainable mountain farming and agrifood systems.
