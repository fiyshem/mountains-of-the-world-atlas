# The Sierra Nevada

Explore granite peaks, glacial valleys, giant sequoia groves, alpine lakes and water systems of California and Nevada.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Granite and uplifted relief

The Sierra Nevada forms a long mountain block along eastern California and nearby Nevada. Its granitic rocks formed from cooled magma deep within the crust and were later exposed through uplift and erosion. Glaciers carved many high valleys and basins during colder periods, while rivers and weathering continue to shape slopes today.

## A vertical ecological journey

Lower foothills may support oak woodland and chaparral; mid-elevations contain conifer forests; higher belts include subalpine woodland, alpine meadows, bare rock and seasonal snow. Giant sequoias occur in specific settings rather than throughout the entire range. Elevation bands shift with aspect, water availability, fire history and local geology.

## Snowpack, rivers and people

Snowpack is an important seasonal water store for multiple basins, while reservoirs and managed water systems connect mountain runoff to agriculture and cities. Drought, wildfire, forest health and shifts in snow timing interact. A winter with little snow is not equivalent to a full annual water assessment, but repeated changes in snowpack affect planning and ecosystems.

## Conservation and recreation

National parks and other protected areas conserve iconic habitats, but roads, visitors, fire, invasive species and climate change pose challenges. Trail design, wilderness management, prescribed fire where appropriate, watershed protection and public education work together. Visitors should follow current fire restrictions and respect closures around sensitive habitats.
