The Alps extend across several European countries, including France, Switzerland, Italy, Austria, Germany, and Slovenia. They contain glacial peaks, limestone cliffs, deep valleys, forests, alpine grasslands, and important river headwaters.

The Alps formed through a complex history of tectonic convergence, folding, faulting, uplift, and erosion. Glaciers carved many of their characteristic valleys and left lakes and deposits that continue to shape local landscapes.

## Mammals

- Alpine ibex (Capra ibex): a wild goat with specialised hooves for climbing steep rocky terrain.
- Chamois (Rupicapra rupicapra): an agile mountain ungulate of rocky slopes, meadows, and forests.
- Alpine marmot (Marmota marmota): a burrowing rodent that hibernates through the cold season.
- Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx): a forest-associated predator that requires suitable prey and connected habitat.
- Red deer (Cervus elaphus): a large herbivore found in woodland and open mountain landscapes.
- Alpine hare (Lepus timidus): a cold-adapted hare with seasonal coat changes in many populations.

## Birds

- Bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus): a specialist scavenger that consumes substantial amounts of bone.
- Alpine chough (Pyrrhocorax graculus): a social corvid often seen around high mountain cliffs and open alpine terrain.
- Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos): a top predator of many alpine landscapes.
- Western capercaillie (Tetrao urogallus): a large forest grouse associated with suitable woodland structure.
- Wallcreeper (Tichodroma muraria): a specialised bird that searches steep rock faces for invertebrates.
- White-winged snowfinch (Montifringilla nivalis): a high-altitude bird adapted to open, rocky mountain environments.

## Trees and plants

- European silver fir (Abies alba): a conifer of montane forests.
- Norway spruce (Picea abies): a major tree of central and northern European forests, including parts of the Alps.
- European larch (Larix decidua): a deciduous conifer that sheds its needles in autumn.
- Swiss stone pine (Pinus cembra): a high-elevation conifer that can form slow-growing mountain woodlands.
- European beech (Fagus sylvatica): an important broadleaf tree in lower and middle elevations where conditions are suitable.
- Edelweiss (Leontopodium nivale): an iconic alpine plant associated with rocky and open mountain habitats.

The Alps are an important example of how protected landscapes, traditional grazing, tourism, transportation infrastructure, and climate change intersect. Alpine species can be especially vulnerable when warming pushes their suitable habitats toward increasingly limited areas at higher elevations.

## Glaciers, valleys and a working landscape

Many classic Alpine valley shapes reflect the work of glaciers that deepened and widened existing valleys. Ice also left moraines, polished rock, overdeepened basins and lakes. Rivers and gravity continue to move sediment, while freeze–thaw action loosens rock on exposed slopes. Reading these features together helps connect present-day scenery to a much longer geological history.

The Alps are also a landscape of long-standing human use. Pastures, villages, transport corridors, hydroelectric infrastructure, ski areas and protected habitats often occupy neighbouring parts of the same valley. Traditional grazing can maintain some open grassland habitats, while intensive development or abandonment may change the structure of others. It is therefore too simple to divide the Alps neatly into untouched wilderness and human space.

## A changing high-mountain climate

Warming can alter the duration of snow cover, the seasonal timing of meltwater, the balance of glacier gain and loss, and the elevation at which plants and animals find suitable conditions. Alpine species already adapted to cold may have limited space to move when the highest available terrain is reached. The effect varies by species and slope, making long-term monitoring and habitat connectivity valuable.

A thoughtful Alpine field study asks how geology, water, biodiversity and land use overlap in one valley. Which slopes face the sun? Where does the stream emerge from a steep gorge? Where does forest give way to pasture or rock? Which parts of the valley are intensively visited, and which remain quiet refuges for wildlife?
