# Mountain Rivers, Watersheds & Freshwater

Follow water from snowfields and springs through headwater streams, wetlands, lakes, reservoirs and downstream plains.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## A watershed is a connected system

A watershed or catchment is the land area that drains toward a common outlet. Ridges form divides; small rills join streams; tributaries combine into rivers. Water reaches a channel through direct runoff, shallow subsurface flow, groundwater and the melting of seasonal snow or glacier ice. The proportions change with geology, vegetation, soil depth, rainfall intensity and season. A watershed is therefore not merely a line on a map but a network of linked processes.

## Headwaters influence what happens downstream

Mountain streams can supply water, sediment, nutrients and organic material to lower catchments. Steep gradients often mean rapid response to heavy rainfall, though storage in wetlands, floodplains, lakes, soils and reservoirs can alter the timing. Sediment is natural and builds habitat, but sudden excess sediment can damage infrastructure or smother aquatic life. Dams, diversions, roads, mines, agriculture and urban growth may change flow, temperature, water quality and connectivity.

## Seasonal flow and competing needs

In snow-dominated basins, snowpack stores water that may be released during spring and summer. Rain-dominated basins respond differently, and glacier-fed rivers can have distinctive seasonal patterns. Climate change, land-use change and water withdrawals can shift both annual totals and the timing of supply. Farming, household use, hydropower, industry and ecosystems may depend on the same water. Fair management needs local measurements, transparent decisions and recognition of both upstream and downstream needs.

## Floods, sediment and river health

Mountain floods may be produced by intense rain, rapid snowmelt, glacier-lake outburst, landslide dams or combinations of events. Flood risk depends on the hazard as well as exposure and vulnerability: a natural flood in an uninhabited valley has different consequences from one reaching a settlement. Wetlands, intact riparian vegetation and room for rivers to shift can support resilience, though no single measure removes all risk.

## Observe a stream carefully

Record location, weather, water level, flow direction, bank condition, vegetation and any visible sediment. A photograph from the same safe viewpoint repeated after storms or across seasons can show change. Never enter swift cold water to collect a measurement without suitable training and equipment. Water clarity does not prove that water is safe to drink; follow local health advice and use appropriate treatment when needed.
