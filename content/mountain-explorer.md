Use the elevation explorer to connect changing height with changing habitats. Mountain belts are not identical everywhere: latitude, moisture, aspect, wind, geology, disturbance, and regional climate can move the boundaries up or down.

## What to look for
As you move from foothills toward the highest ridges, tree composition often changes, trees become shorter and more widely spaced, and continuous forest may give way to alpine shrubs, grasses, cushion plants, lichens, bare rock, snow, and ice. Some tropical mountains have striking alpine zones despite their latitude; some dry ranges have a high and fragmented tree line.

## Think in systems
Elevation influences temperature and growing season, but species also need suitable soil, moisture, food, shelter, and connections to other habitats. A warming climate may allow some species to shift upward, yet summit area shrinks with height. Roads, farms, settlements, and fragmented habitat can make movement harder.

## A simplified gradient, not a global rule

The elevation slider uses broad illustrative bands so you can test the concept interactively. It does not predict the tree line of a specific mountain. At lower latitudes, high elevations can be cool enough to support alpine plants above tropical forest; in dry mountains, tree cover may be limited by water long before cold becomes the main constraint. Moisture, slope aspect, wind, geology, fire and grazing can shift the transition lines.

## Track linked changes

When considering climate change, avoid thinking only about a single species moving uphill. Changes in snow cover influence meltwater timing; plants alter the food and shelter available to animals; a forest transition can affect soils, stream shade and fire behaviour. Some species may expand upward, others may lose suitable habitat, and some cannot move because the next suitable patch is isolated by roads, farmland, cliffs or unsuitable climate.

Try to use the explorer as a starting question: which variables would you measure on a real mountain? A strong study would record temperature, moisture, aspect, soil, plant structure and evidence of disturbance at multiple elevations, then repeat the observations across seasons and years.
