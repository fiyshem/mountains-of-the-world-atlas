# Climate Change in Mountain Regions

Explore observed shifts in snow, glaciers, permafrost, species ranges and hazards, alongside adaptation choices for communities and ecosystems.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Why mountain change is highly visible

Mountains contain strong environmental gradients over short distances, and many communities depend on snow, ice, forests and headwater streams. Warming can reduce snow-cover duration at some elevations, contribute to glacier mass loss, thaw permafrost and change growing seasons. The details vary across ranges and elevation bands. Local precipitation, aerosols, land use and topography interact with global warming, so regional assessments matter.

## The cryosphere and water timing

Snowpack and glacier ice store water that is released later. Where glaciers shrink, runoff may initially change as melt increases, but the long-term supply can decline once less ice remains. Rainfall shifts may matter as much as temperature. Floods, droughts and seasonal mismatches between supply and demand can affect irrigation, hydropower, habitats and drinking-water systems differently in each basin.

## Biodiversity and livelihoods

Some plants and animals shift their distributions upslope as conditions change, but movement can be blocked by cliffs, fragmented habitat, competition or lack of suitable soil. Summit specialists may run out of space. Farm calendars, pests, pasture quality and forest disturbance can change too. Impacts are uneven: some locations may experience new opportunities while others face severe loss and increased uncertainty.

## Hazard interactions

Warming can thaw frozen ground, destabilise rock walls, alter snowpack and contribute to glacier-lake changes. Heavy rainfall and wildfire can interact with steep terrain to increase debris-flow or erosion risk. It is rarely accurate to attribute a specific disaster to one driver without event-specific analysis. Risk reduction should combine climate information, geological assessment, land-use planning, early warning and community preparedness.

## Adaptation with local leadership

Options include protecting headwater wetlands, diversifying water storage and supply, restoring connected habitat, adjusting farm practices, updating hazard maps and designing flexible infrastructure. No measure fits every valley, and adaptation may have limits where change is rapid or resources are scarce. The IPCC’s mountain assessment summarises observed impacts, projected risks and adaptation responses; it also emphasises the importance of connected human and ecological systems.
