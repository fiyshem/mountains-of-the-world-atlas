The Andes extend along the western edge of South America, crossing several countries from the continent's northern regions to Patagonia. They include some of the world's highest mountains outside Asia and contain volcanoes, high plateaus, cloud forests, glaciers, deserts, and grasslands.

Much of the Andes developed through the subduction of oceanic crust beneath the South American Plate, accompanied by volcanic activity, crustal deformation, and uplift. The range influences rainfall across the continent and creates major differences between the humid eastern slopes and the drier western side.

## Mammals

Spectacled bear (Tremarctos ornatus)

South America's only living native bear species. It inhabits Andean forests and adjacent habitats, feeding on a varied diet of plant material and animal foods. Forest continuity is important for its survival.

Vicuña (Vicugna vicugna)

A wild camelid adapted to high-altitude grasslands and puna environments. Its fine fleece and specialised physiology are associated with life in cold, open Andean landscapes.

Andean mountain cat (Leopardus jacobita)

A rare small wild cat associated with rocky high-altitude environments. Its survival is linked to suitable habitat and the availability of prey such as mountain viscachas.

Guanaco (Lama guanicoe)

A wild South American camelid that occupies grasslands, shrublands, and open mountain landscapes. Its grazing affects vegetation and links plant production to larger predators and scavengers.

## Birds

- Andean condor (Vultur gryphus): a large scavenger that uses mountain updrafts to soar efficiently over extensive landscapes.
- Andean cock-of-the-rock (Rupicola peruvianus): a striking bird of humid Andean forests, known for communal courtship displays.
- Torrent duck (Merganetta armata): a specialised waterbird of fast-flowing mountain streams.
- Andean goose (Chloephaga melanoptera): a high-altitude waterbird that uses wet grasslands and mountain lakes.
- Puna tinamou (Tinamotis pentlandii): a ground-dwelling bird of open high-elevation habitats.
- Giant hummingbird (Patagona gigas): an unusually large hummingbird that uses nectar-rich flowers and other food resources in Andean environments.

## Trees and plants

- Polylepis trees: a group of high-Andean trees notable for their ability to grow near the upper limits of woody vegetation in parts of the range.
- Queñua woodlands: high-altitude woodland communities formed by Polylepis species.
- Quinine trees (Cinchona species): tropical montane trees with historical importance because their bark contains quinine-related compounds.
- Cloud-forest orchids: diverse flowering plants associated with humid, misty mountain forests.
- Puya bromeliads: striking plants of some Andean grasslands and rocky slopes.
- Puna grasses and cushion plants: vegetation adapted to cold nights, intense sunlight, and limited water.

## Mountain ecology

The Andes demonstrate how mountains can generate biodiversity through isolation and environmental gradients. Populations separated by valleys, ridges, and different climate zones may evolve independently.

The high Andes also show how ancient landscapes and modern climate processes interact. Glacial retreat, changing precipitation, grazing, mining, fire, and land conversion can all affect the stability of mountain ecosystems.

## Water, elevation and the dry-side contrast

A useful way to read the Andes is to trace water across a cross-section of the range. Moisture-bearing air, high ridges and slope orientation help create sharp differences between neighbouring valleys. Some slopes support humid montane or cloud forest, while high interior plateaus are colder and drier. In the western Andes, arid landscapes can sit surprisingly close to high snow and ice because the regional circulation and rain-shadow effect limit precipitation in many places.

The puna is a high-elevation grassland and shrubland region rather than a single uniform habitat. Its vegetation is adapted to cold nights, strong sunlight and seasonal moisture. Wetlands, streams and shallow lakes can form important oases for wildlife and people in otherwise open terrain. Polylepis woodlands, where they persist, are particularly interesting because these trees can grow near the upper limit of woody vegetation and form habitat islands among grasslands and rocky slopes.

## People, landscapes and changing pressures

Andean landscapes have long supported agriculture, grazing, transport routes and settlements at elevations that can challenge visitors from lower altitudes. Local knowledge reflects careful adaptation to slope, soil, water availability and seasonal weather. Modern pressures vary from place to place and may include mining, road building, changing grazing patterns, fire, land conversion and glacier retreat.

A productive field question is to compare a wet upper catchment with a dry downstream valley. Look at how vegetation, water access, soil stability and settlement patterns change, then investigate which differences result from elevation, which from rainfall, and which from land use. This systems approach reveals why the Andes matter far beyond the summit skyline.
