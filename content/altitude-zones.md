One of the most important concepts in mountain ecology is that a single mountain can contain several different ecosystems stacked above one another.

As elevation increases, temperatures generally decrease, growing seasons shorten, and exposure to wind and ultraviolet radiation often increases. Rainfall patterns also change according to slope orientation, prevailing winds, latitude, and local geography.

The exact boundaries between vegetation zones vary considerably. A tropical mountain may support cloud forest at elevations where a mountain in a temperate region has coniferous forest or alpine grassland.

## 4.1 Foothills and lower montane forests

Foothills connect mountain systems to surrounding plains, plateaus, and lowland forests. They may support tropical rainforest, subtropical woodland, temperate broadleaf forest, or dry scrub depending on geography and climate.

Typical plants: oak, maple, chestnut, magnolia, bamboo, palms in some tropical regions, and many climbing plants.

Typical mammals: deer, wild boar, monkeys in suitable Asian and African habitats, foxes, small carnivores, and bears in some temperate ranges.

Typical birds: woodpeckers, thrushes, hornbills in parts of Asia, forest raptors, pigeons, and numerous insect-eating songbirds.

These lower elevations often have high biological productivity and support many species that cannot survive in the colder environments above.

## 4.2 Montane forests

Montane forests occupy middle elevations where moisture and temperature support substantial tree growth. They may contain broadleaf trees, conifers, or mixed forests.

Trees create layered habitats: the canopy receives strong light, the understory provides shelter, and fallen wood supports fungi, insects, amphibians, and small mammals.

Typical inhabitants include squirrels, martens, deer, bears, owls, woodpeckers, and many forest songbirds.

Important ecological processes include decomposition, nutrient cycling, seed dispersal, natural regeneration, and the movement of water through forest soils.

## 4.3 Cloud forests

Cloud forests occur in persistently humid mountain environments where frequent cloud or mist influences vegetation. They are particularly prominent in tropical and subtropical mountain regions.

Mosses, ferns, orchids, bromeliads, and other epiphytes can grow on branches and trunks. The combination of high humidity and complex vegetation creates specialised habitats for amphibians, insects, birds, and mammals.

Cloud forests can capture moisture from wind-driven fog, although the amount varies by site. Changes in cloud formation, temperature, and land cover may alter their water balance.

## 4.4 Subalpine forests

Subalpine forests occur near the upper limits of continuous forest growth in many mountain systems. Short growing seasons, snow loading, cold temperatures, and strong winds influence tree form and distribution.

Common trees include spruce, fir, larch, mountain pine, and other locally adapted conifers.

Animals may include mountain goats, marmots, pikas, grouse, and predators that move between forest and open habitats. Some species use the forest as shelter while feeding in nearby meadows.

## 4.5 The tree line

The tree line marks the upper boundary beyond which trees cannot generally grow as a continuous forest under the prevailing environmental conditions.

It is not a perfectly sharp line. Isolated trees, krummholz (stunted, wind-shaped trees), and scattered shrubs may occur above or near the boundary.

Temperature is important, but wind exposure, snow duration, soil conditions, moisture, and growing-season length also influence tree growth.

The tree line differs by latitude, mountain range, slope orientation, and local climate. It is therefore not appropriate to assign one universal altitude to it.

## 4.6 Alpine grasslands and meadows

Above the tree line, many mountains support grasses, sedges, cushion plants, low shrubs, and flowering herbs.

These plants often grow close to the ground, reducing exposure to wind and conserving heat. Some produce flowers rapidly during the short growing season, while others survive for years through underground structures.

Mountain ungulates graze in these habitats, and insects pollinate flowers. Birds such as snowfinches, ptarmigans, and mountain accentors use alpine landscapes in different regions.

Alpine meadows are sensitive to trampling, excessive grazing, altered snow cover, invasive species, and climate change.

## 4.7 Nival zones, glaciers, and permanent snowfields

At the highest elevations, persistent snow, ice, exposed rock, and extremely short growing seasons limit terrestrial life.

Even these environments are not completely lifeless. Microorganisms, specialised algae, lichens, and small invertebrates can survive in suitable ice, rock, and snow-associated habitats.

Birds may fly over high summits or forage on exposed slopes, while mountain mammals may occasionally cross high passes. However, the highest icy terrain generally supports far less terrestrial biological activity than the forests and meadows below.

Glaciers and snowfields also influence water supplies, sediment transport, and downstream ecosystems.
