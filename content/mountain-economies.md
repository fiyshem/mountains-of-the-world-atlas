# Mountain Economies & Ecosystem Services

Examine freshwater, farming, forests, tourism, mining, hydropower and the economic dependencies linking uplands with downstream regions.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Benefits travel beyond the peaks

Mountain ecosystems can store and release freshwater, support biodiversity, regulate some local climate processes, stabilise soils and offer cultural and recreational value. These benefits—often called **ecosystem services**—extend to people who do not live in mountain regions. Their value is not only a market price: species, customary landscapes and sacred places may carry cultural and intrinsic importance that money cannot capture.

## Many economies share the same landscape

Mountain livelihoods may include agriculture, pastoralism, forest products, tourism, hydropower, construction, mining, transport and public services. These activities can provide income and infrastructure but may compete for land, water and habitat. A new road may lower travel costs yet fragment habitat; a reservoir may produce electricity yet alter river flow; tourism may create jobs while increasing waste and seasonal prices. Each trade-off needs local evidence.

## Who receives the benefit—and who bears the cost?

Benefits and risks can fall on different groups. Downstream cities may use water generated in upper catchments, while mountain communities face land restrictions, hazards or reduced access. Revenues may leave the region if ownership and procurement are external. Transparent consultation, fair compensation, public services, local purchasing and participation in decisions can help address these imbalances, although no single policy solves every conflict.

## Value, measurement and uncertainty

Economic appraisals may estimate avoided flood damage, water regulation, tourism income or carbon storage. Results depend on assumptions, baseline conditions, time horizon and the values included. A benefit that is difficult to price should not automatically be treated as worthless. Scenario analysis and multi-criteria assessment can reveal ecological and cultural costs that a simple financial total would hide.

## Toward durable livelihoods

Economic resilience often rests on diversity, reliable infrastructure, education, local skills and healthy ecosystems. Sustainable development aims to maintain resources and opportunities without transferring unmanageable costs to future generations. The FAO Mountain Partnership provides material on agrifood systems, water, forests and the role mountain regions play in connected landscapes.
