# Wildlife Corridors & Landscape Connectivity

Understand how ridgelines, valleys, stepping-stone habitats and safe crossings help animals move across changing mountain landscapes.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## A habitat is not useful if it cannot be reached

A protected area can retain good habitat but still become isolated by roads, fences, settlements, agriculture or cleared land. Connectivity describes how easily organisms can move between habitat patches to find food, mates, seasonal ranges and new territories. Different species perceive the same landscape differently: a road may be a minor barrier to one bird but a severe barrier to a ground-dwelling amphibian.

## Corridors and stepping stones

A corridor may be a continuous strip of suitable habitat, while stepping stones are smaller patches that support movement across a broader matrix. River valleys, forest belts, rocky ridges and elevational gradients can become important routes. Corridor placement should be based on species ecology, landscape structure and verified movement data rather than drawing a line between two protected areas and assuming it will work.

## Roads, crossings and conflict

Wildlife overpasses, underpasses, fencing and speed management can reduce some road-related impacts when designed for the target species and maintained. Poorly located crossings may go unused. Habitat connectivity can also reduce isolation caused by climate change, allowing populations to shift toward suitable conditions. At the same time, movement can spread disease or invasive species, so connectivity plans need ecological context.

## Evidence for planning

Camera traps, track surveys, GPS tags, genetic studies, roadkill records and local knowledge can each reveal different aspects of movement. Every method has limits: cameras miss animals outside their field of view; tags cover few individuals; and genetics often describes movement over longer periods. Combining evidence can identify where a barrier matters and whether an intervention changes outcomes.

## Connectivity is social too

Land ownership, livelihoods, transport needs and community safety shape what corridor plans are possible. Conservation works best when people who live with wildlife take part in defining priorities and sharing benefits. Public maps should avoid revealing the exact locations of highly vulnerable species when disclosure could increase disturbance or illegal collection.
