Location: Afghanistan–Pakistan border regions, India, Nepal, Bhutan, and China, depending on the range and regional definition.

The Himalayas form one of the world's most remarkable mountain systems. They developed through the ongoing convergence of the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates, which uplifted enormous volumes of rock and continue to shape the region's geology. The Himalayas contain Mount Everest, the highest mountain above sea level, and numerous other peaks exceeding 8,000 metres.

The region includes subtropical foothills, deep river valleys, temperate forests, coniferous woodlands, alpine meadows, glaciers, and cold high-altitude deserts. This variety creates a wide range of habitats and supports species with very different ecological requirements. UNESCO documents this exceptional diversity in protected areas such as Great Himalayan National Park and Khangchendzonga National Park.&#x20;

## Mammals of the Himalayas

Snow leopard (Panthera uncia)

A specialised mountain cat with dense fur, broad paws, and a long tail used for balance and warmth. It hunts mountain ungulates such as blue sheep and ibex. Its camouflage and remote habitat make it difficult to observe in the wild.

Red panda (Ailurus fulgens)

A forest-dwelling mammal associated with cool, humid montane forests containing bamboo. Although it belongs to its own family, Ailuridae, it shares certain ecological requirements with other forest specialists, including access to suitable tree cover and food plants.

Himalayan tahr (Hemitragus jemlahicus)

A sturdy mountain ungulate adapted to steep terrain. Its hooves help it move across rocky slopes, and its diet includes grasses, shrubs, and other mountain vegetation.

Blue sheep or bharal (Pseudois nayaur)

An agile mountain ungulate that grazes on alpine slopes. It is an important prey species for snow leopards in parts of the Himalayas.

Himalayan musk deer (Moschus chrysogaster)

A shy forest and mountain animal. Unlike true deer in the family Cervidae, musk deer belong to Moschidae; males of relevant species possess elongated upper canine teeth rather than antlers.

## Birds of the Himalayas

- Himalayan monal (Lophophorus impejanus): a vividly coloured pheasant of Himalayan forests and alpine slopes.
- Western tragopan (Tragopan melanocephalus): an elusive pheasant associated with temperate forests and dense vegetation.
- Bearded vulture (Gypaetus barbatus): a large scavenger known for its specialised use of bones as food.
- Golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos): a powerful raptor that hunts mammals and birds in open mountain terrain.
- Snow partridge (Lerwa lerwa): a ground-dwelling bird of high-altitude rocky grasslands and slopes.
- Grandala (Grandala coelicolor): a high-altitude passerine known for the striking blue plumage of males.
- Blood pheasant (Ithaginis cruentus): a pheasant of high-elevation forests and scrub in parts of the eastern Himalayas.

## Trees and plants

The Himalayas have several distinctive plant communities:

- Himalayan blue pine (Pinus wallichiana): a conifer of montane forests, recognised by its long, slender needles.
- Himalayan cedar or deodar (Cedrus deodara): a large conifer of the western Himalayas.
- West Himalayan fir (Abies pindrow): an evergreen conifer found in cool montane forests.
- Himalayan birch (Betula utilis): a high-elevation tree whose pale bark is a characteristic feature of many upper mountain woodlands.
- Rhododendrons: a diverse group of shrubs and trees, some of which dominate colourful mountain slopes during flowering seasons.
- Junipers: woody plants that can survive exposed, cold, and relatively dry high-altitude conditions.
- Alpine gentians and primulas: flowering plants adapted to short growing seasons and harsh mountain weather.

## Key ecological relationships

The Himalayan ecosystem illustrates how elevation connects different habitats. Forests provide shelter and food for numerous birds and mammals, while alpine meadows support grazing ungulates. Predators depend on the distribution and abundance of prey, and the condition of headwater catchments affects downstream rivers and communities.

Major conservation concerns include habitat fragmentation, infrastructure development, illegal hunting, human–wildlife conflict, and climate-driven changes in snow and glacier systems. Species distributions vary across the range; not every Himalayan animal occurs throughout the entire mountain system.
