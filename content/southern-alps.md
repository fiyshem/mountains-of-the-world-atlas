The Southern Alps form the main mountain backbone of New Zealand's South Island. The range contains steep peaks, glaciers, glacial lakes, alpine herbfields, beech forests, and fast-flowing rivers.

Its ecology reflects isolation, strong rainfall gradients, glacial history, and the evolution of distinctive New Zealand species. Native plants and animals developed in an island environment with a different history of terrestrial predators from many continental mountain systems.

## Animals and birds

- Kea (Nestor notabilis): an alpine parrot with flexible foraging behaviour and a reputation for exploratory problem-solving.
- Rock wren (Xenicus gilviventris): a small bird associated with alpine rock fields and mountain vegetation.
- New Zealand falcon (Falco novaeseelandiae): a native raptor that hunts birds and other prey in varied landscapes.
- Tahr (Hemitragus jemlahicus): an introduced mountain ungulate that can influence alpine vegetation through browsing.
- Chamois (Rupicapra rupicapra): another introduced ungulate established in parts of New Zealand.
- Long-tailed bat (Chalinolobus tuberculatus): a native mammal found in some forested landscapes.

## Trees and plants

- Mountain beech (Fuscospora cliffortioides): a tree of montane and subalpine forests.
- Silver beech (Lophozonia menziesii): a native forest tree of cooler, often wetter environments.
- Dracophyllum species: distinctive shrubs and small trees found in a variety of New Zealand habitats.
- Alpine buttercups (Ranunculus species): flowering plants that include specialised alpine forms.
- Mountain daisies and cushion plants: plants adapted to wind, cold, and short growing seasons.

Conservation challenges include introduced mammals, browsing pressure, habitat change, and climate-driven changes in glaciers and alpine environments. The range also offers excellent opportunities to study how isolation influences evolution.

## Rain, ice and steep ecological gradients

The Southern Alps rise within a maritime island climate. Moist air crossing the Tasman Sea encounters the mountain barrier, producing strong contrasts in rainfall between exposed western slopes and areas farther east. This helps explain the close juxtaposition of wet forest, glacial valleys, alpine grassland and drier inland basins. Elevation is important, but the direction of prevailing weather is also a major influence on the landscape.

Glaciers and rivers continually shape the range. Ice can carve steep-sided valleys and leave moraines, while streams transport rock fragments toward lakes and lowland plains. Snow and ice influence both high-alpine habitats and water systems downstream. The pace and pattern of change differ between catchments, so comparisons are strongest when they use observations from a named valley and a defined period.

## Island biodiversity and introduced species

New Zealand’s long geographic isolation produced a distinctive evolutionary history, including native birds and plants adapted to local forest and alpine habitats. The kea and rock wren represent very different ways of living in high terrain: one is a highly mobile alpine parrot, while the other is a small bird associated with rocky alpine environments. Introduced mammals and ungulates can create additional pressures through predation or browsing, with outcomes that vary by habitat and species.

A useful field question is how a forest edge transitions into alpine herbfield. Compare plant height, exposure, soil depth, wind and signs of browsing. Because island ecosystems have distinctive histories, conservation decisions should be grounded in New Zealand-specific evidence rather than assumptions imported from continental mountain ranges.
