# Mountain Weather & Microclimates

Understand orographic rain, valley winds, inversions, foehn-like downslope warming, thunderstorms and rapid changes with terrain.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## Why mountains make weather complicated

Atmospheric flow must move around or over terrain. As moist air rises along a slope, it expands and cools; if it reaches saturation, cloud and precipitation may form. The windward slope can be wet while descending air on the leeward side is warmer and drier, creating a rain shadow. The strength of these effects depends on wind direction, stability, moisture and the shape of the range. A broad regional forecast may miss important local differences.

## Daily valley and slope winds

Sunlight warms slopes during the day, often encouraging upslope and up-valley winds. At night, cooled dense air can drain downslope and collect in valley bottoms. Under some conditions, a temperature inversion places colder air below warmer air, trapping fog or pollutants. These patterns are modified by cloud, snow cover, vegetation, valley orientation and large-scale winds. Local winds can reverse or break down when weather systems change.

## Clouds, storms and exposure

Ridges and peaks can trigger turbulence, cloud formation and rapid changes in visibility. Convective thunderstorms can develop on warm unstable days; lightning risk increases for exposed ridgelines and isolated high points. Strong wind accelerates heat loss and can make footing difficult. Wet snow, hail, freezing rain, blowing snow and cloud immersion may occur in combinations. Weather can differ substantially between the trailhead and the summit.

## Read forecasts as decisions, not guarantees

Check a forecast close to departure, including expected wind, precipitation type, freezing level, thunderstorm timing and visibility. Compare more than one trusted local source when available. Identify a turnaround time and safe exit options before conditions deteriorate. Forecast uncertainty generally grows with lead time and at very local scales. A forecast is a planning tool, not permission to ignore what the sky, wind and terrain are doing.

## A field weather log

Record time, elevation, cloud cover, wind direction, precipitation, temperature if available and the exact location. Use consistent methods when comparing sites. Avoid placing instruments in direct sun or beside heat sources if you want representative air-temperature readings. Never attempt to measure weather from an exposed summit during an approaching storm. Safety comes first; observations can be repeated later from a protected place.
