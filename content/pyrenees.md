# The Pyrenees

Explore the mountain barrier between the Iberian Peninsula and the rest of Europe, with alpine habitats, pastoral landscapes and varied geology.

> **Atlas note:** This article is a learning introduction. Conditions differ across mountain ranges; use regional sources for local decisions.

## A boundary with many crossings

The Pyrenees extend between the Atlantic side of the Bay of Biscay and the Mediterranean, forming a major mountain barrier between France and Spain and including Andorra. Passes, valleys and river corridors have long connected communities across the range. The boundary is not a sealed wall: languages, trade, seasonal grazing and movement have linked both sides for centuries.

## Geology and relief

The range records the convergence and deformation associated with the Iberian and Eurasian plates, along with older rocks and later uplift and erosion. Granite massifs, folded sedimentary units and limestone terrain create different landforms. Glaciers shaped high valleys and cirques during colder periods; remaining small glaciers and perennial snow features are sensitive to temperature and local topography.

## Habitats and wildlife

Lower elevations include woodland and human-shaped pasture; higher slopes support beech, fir and pine forests, subalpine scrub and alpine grassland. Species vary across the range and between protected areas. Large carnivores, ungulates and specialised birds depend on suitable habitat and connected movement routes, while traditional grazing can maintain open habitats when matched to local conditions.

## People, land use and conservation

Tourism, skiing, hiking, livestock, transport and hydropower shape the contemporary Pyrenees. Conservation questions include habitat fragmentation, changing snow seasons, wildfire, water management and the balance between recreation and quiet habitat. Useful study compares the French, Spanish and Andorran contexts rather than treating the range as one uniform landscape.
