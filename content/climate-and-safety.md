Mountain weather can change quickly because elevation, slope orientation, wind, cloud, and local terrain interact. A sunny valley does not guarantee safe conditions on an exposed ridge, and conditions can differ sharply across nearby watersheds.

## Before setting out
Check a forecast that covers the elevation and area you plan to visit, learn about local closures and seasonal hazards, tell someone your route, and carry equipment appropriate to the terrain and expected conditions. Set a clear turnaround time and change plans when the forecast or conditions deteriorate.

## Weather and exposure
- **Wind:** exposed ridges can feel much colder than sheltered valleys and can make balance difficult.
- **Rain and thunderstorms:** wet rock can become slippery; lightning is especially dangerous on open ridges and summits.
- **Snow and ice:** snow bridges, cornices, hidden creeks, and icy paths can conceal serious hazards.
- **Cold and heat:** dress in layers and protect yourself from wind, sun, and rapid temperature changes.
- **Visibility:** fog, blowing snow, and darkness can make route-finding difficult even on familiar terrain.

## Terrain hazards
Steep loose slopes, rockfall, landslides, avalanches, swollen streams, and glacial terrain require specific local knowledge and training. Do not enter avalanche terrain without appropriate education, equipment, partners, and a current regional avalanche forecast. Never approach glacier edges or snow cornices for a photograph.

## Altitude and pacing
Higher elevations generally mean lower atmospheric pressure and less oxygen available with each breath. Ascend gradually where possible, allow time to adapt, and take symptoms seriously. This is general educational information, not medical advice; consult a qualified clinician about personal risks before high-altitude travel. Severe or worsening symptoms require prompt descent and emergency assistance.

## Leave the landscape better
Stay on durable paths, pack out waste, keep wildlife at a distance, avoid feeding animals, minimise noise, and follow local fire and camping rules. Mountain ecosystems often recover slowly from trampling, erosion, and disturbance.

## A final decision check
Ask: Is the forecast suitable? Does everyone feel well? Is the route within our skills? Do we have the right equipment and a way to communicate? What is our safer alternative? Turning around is sound mountain judgement, not failure.

## Plan for the return as well as the ascent

A route is only manageable if the group has enough time and energy to return safely. Estimate travel time conservatively, account for slower progress on uneven or steep terrain, and identify escape routes or sheltered stopping points before setting out. Leave a route plan with a trusted person where appropriate. Mobile coverage and navigation apps can fail, especially in valleys, storms or remote areas, so do not rely on a single device.

## Know the local warnings

Mountain conditions are highly regional. Avalanche danger, wildfire restrictions, flood warnings, glacier access rules and trail closures can change over short periods. Check official information for the exact area and date, and follow instructions from park staff, rescue services and local authorities. This page is a general learning resource, not a substitute for formal training, a qualified guide or a location-specific forecast.

## Make decisions before the weather forces them

Agree on turnaround criteria before the group becomes tired or committed to a summit. Examples include a specific time, worsening cloud, increasing wind, unstable snow, rising stream levels, or symptoms of illness. Reassess the plan as conditions change. If someone in the group feels unwell, cold, overwhelmed or unsafe, treat that as useful information rather than a challenge to overcome.
